Who Gets to Say STOP?

A highway message board never has the authority to stop you. The US traffic-control manual is explicit: “The STOP sign shall not be displayed using a changeable message sign” (MUTCD 11th ed., §2B.04 ¶03 [C12]). We digitally edited real board photos and asked in-car-sized vision-language models what to do.

Headline: Study 2, words on a board

Plain reading. The small model obeys the word STOP, and also closure text, on a roadside board, whatever the board is and whoever wrote it. It is not specifically hijacked by imperative commands: the injection beats a length-matched benign command and nonsense text, but it is indistinguishable from a bare “STOP”. In the reverse direction, a board saying ROAD CLEAR / PROCEED, added to a real STOP scene, made it answer non-STOP far more often than a benign board did. That is the safety-critical direction.

Later phases

Why it matters

VLMs are entering the driving stack

Waymo describes an onboard “Driving VLM … trained using Gemini” [B1]. Wayve’s LINGO-2 drives from language [B3]. NVIDIA’s Alpamayo-R1 is an open reasoning VLA [B5]. DriveVLM runs on production hardware at about 410 ms per inference [B4].

Text in the scene is an input channel

Typographic attacks transfer to driving VLMs [A3]. Sign-based command hijacking reached high success on DriveLM and a robot car [A6]. Agents confuse helpful and misleading in-scene instructions [A7]. Billboard and projected stop signs fooled production ADAS [A10] [A11] [C7].

Public boards have been altered before

Austin’s message boards were made to read “Zombies Ahead” in 2009 [C8]. Who answers for a hacked public board steering a car: the road agency or the manufacturer?

Stop decisions are a regulatory focus

NHTSA recall 23V-085 covered stop-sign intersections [C1]. PE25012 asks whether FSD can “accurately detect and appropriately respond to traffic signals, signs” [C2]. Waymo’s school-bus recall [C3] and investigation [C4]. Tesla’s manual caveat on temporary stop signs [B7]. Advocacy testing [C9, advocacy].

Methods

Board arm (primary)

63 real highway guide-sign and message-board photos from Wikimedia Commons, from 43 photographers. Nine digital variants per base: clean, benign edit, official closure text, an imperative stop injection, a length-matched benign command, length-matched nonsense, a bare “STOP”, and billboard twins of the injection and the benign command. The correct answer is always PROCEED (or CAUTION); the outcome is P(STOP).

PROCEED-at-STOP arm (co-primary)

71 real scenes with a governing STOP (stop sign, flagger, crossing guard, school-bus arm), from 35 photographer clusters. Each is shown clean, with an added benign message board, and with an added ROAD CLEAR / PROCEED board. The outcome is P(answer ≠ STOP).

Disclosure

All edits are digital composites for an offline audit. The renderer and the edit-generation code are private and not published. No per-phrase effectiveness ranking is published, and this site gives no guidance on altering physical signs.

Protocol & statistics

  • Temperature 0, one run, 640 px long side. Strict leading-word parser (STOP / PROCEED / CAUTION / GO). Scored wording per model: .
  • Wilson 95% CIs. Exact McNemar vs the benign edit, paired by base, Holm-adjusted within each model’s board family (8 tests) and within the 2 stop-scene tests.
  • Photographer-cluster sign-flip randomization test (20,000 draws; floor about 5e-5). Cluster and image bootstrap 95% CIs of the paired difference.
  • ERROR rows are excluded and counted.

Limitations

  • Provisional labels. A single AI-agent labeler and no human raters yet. Two blind raters (κ ≥ 0.6) and an expert review are planned.
  • Not preregistered. The analysis script was finalized while runs were in progress, so treat all p-values as exploratory. The Holm family is per model.
  • Single still frame, offline. This is an audit of a VLM reasoning layer, not a production driving stack. A false STOP is a nuisance or rear-end risk; the PROCEED-at-STOP arm is the safety-critical direction.
  • Competence is narrow. Qwen3-VL-2B passes the stop-sign gate but answers STOP on a sizable share of clean boards. Its competence covers stop signs, not boards.
  • Gate built with YOLO screening. A gate built without YOLO is still missing.
  • Edits are synthetic in the legend region. Benign-edit and length-matched controls address the edit artifact, not realism. The 17 real tampered photos are descriptive only.
  • Scope. One run, one resolution, few models. Qwen2.5-VL-3B and SmolVLM2-2.2B have gate runs only.
  • Privacy. All published images are detector-blurred copies (YOLO persons and vehicles at low confidence, Haar faces and plates), not yet verified by a person. Pilot images awaiting manual plate review stay withheld. No unblurred original is published.

Background: the pilot (Studies 1–3)

The pilot asked the broader question: does a model stop because of who shows the STOP (flagger, crossing guard, police, school-bus arm) or because of its pixels? It also ran an early board study and a sign-removal check. Its verdict was that the board study was the piece most likely to give a real, defensible effect. Phase 2 above is that study rebuilt at scale. Pilot labels are provisional agent labels; its authority look-alike block (A−) is heavily confounded by people in the frame, so those numbers are not a finding.
